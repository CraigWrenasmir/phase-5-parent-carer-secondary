### Slide 4

The two sides of this slide separate understanding, information coming in, from expressing, messages going out. How much someone speaks doesn't tell us how much they understand. A person may speak fluently and still need support to understand spoken information. Someone else may understand more than they can readily express.

A A C means augmentative and alternative communication. It can include signs, a communication board or book, typing or a communication device. Some people use it alongside speech, and some instead of speech. Communication can change with the setting, the demands and how someone is feeling. Repeated words or familiar scripts can carry meaning too. Stay curious about their purpose and the context.

### Slide 10

Connection involves understanding in both directions. The double empathy idea, introduced by Autistic researcher Damian Milton, asks us to consider misunderstandings between people with different experiences and communication styles as a shared gap.

The image shows two people sending and receiving in their own ways. Both have something to understand. You may already do this at home as you learn your young person's communication and they learn yours. We can ask what we, as communication partners, might change to make the exchange easier. Listening and adjustment belong on both sides.

### Slide 14

Now let's look at daily capacity. Something can be enjoyable and still take energy. Communication, sensory processing, transitions and social connection all draw on the resources available in that moment.

### Slide 15

Think of energy as a battery. Each day can begin with a different amount of charge, and activities draw on it. When it runs low, ordinary things can become harder. Many people also use the spoons analogy: a limited supply of energy, with different activities using different amounts.

These are optional ways to describe an experience, rather than measures of ability. The important question is what costs energy and what helps restore it. Rest, familiar interests or a better-fitting environment may help recharge. Support needs to match the charge that is actually available, not the amount we hoped would be there.

### Slide 16

Capacity can vary across a day and from one day to another. Stress, sensory demands, sleep, social situations and other events can change what feels manageable. The same request may be easy on one occasion and too much on another.

Think about what seems to use your young person's energy and what lightens the load. We don't need to interpret a change in capacity as unwillingness. It gives us a reason to look again at the demands and the support available. In secondary school, a timetable with several teachers, assessment due dates and friendship demands can all add to that load.

### Slide 19

The teenage years can add pressure to fit in. A young person may spend more energy hiding parts of themselves while also trying to work out who they are. The effort behind appearing fine can grow across school and social life.

For some young people, sustained overload contributes to burnout or makes school inaccessible. We'll explore those ideas next session. There is also room for growth: when home and school feel safer, young people can develop self-understanding and speak up about what helps. Stay open to their account, and take concerns about wellbeing seriously. You do not have to work those concerns out alone.

### Slide 21

Across these two sessions, we've been building a picture of your young person. How the world arrives through their senses. How they notice detail, focus deeply and bring particular strengths. How they experience internal signals, communicate and reach towards other people.

Managing those demands every day can take a great deal of energy, especially when the surroundings don't fit. Sensory load, transitions, communication across different styles, and sometimes appearing fine can all add to the cost.

A young person who knows who they are, and feels valued for it, has more room for learning, connection and self-understanding. Belonging matters. So does genuine choice about how they communicate, where they feel comfortable and when they need a break. Knowing other Autistic people can offer connection and a sense of shared experience.

Think back to the strengths and joys you wrote down this morning. Supporting a positive Autistic identity can begin with listening, making room for those strengths, and showing that this whole person is worth knowing.
