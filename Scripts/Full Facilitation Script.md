### Slide 3

Sensory processing is the way the brain receives, processes and responds to information from the senses. We often notice the response first, but there is a whole process happening before that response becomes visible.

### Slide 4

Many of us learnt five senses: touch, vision, taste, hearing and smell. Here we include three more. Proprioception is our sense of the body in space, drawing on information from muscles and joints. It helps us know where our body is and how much force we're using.

The vestibular sense relates to balance and movement. Interoception is our sense of internal body signals, such as hunger, thirst or needing the toilet. We'll return to that later. These senses work together. In a busy routine, someone may be managing several kinds of sensory information at once, including input that others barely notice.

Source and adaptation: Parents and Carers - Secondary, Session 2, slide 4; matching slide PDF page 4; PC Secondary - Facilitator Guide PDF page 29; full speaker notes. Authored audience-facing delivery, with optional participation and no invented participant responses.

### Slide 5

Think about taking off a jumper because you're getting hot. First, information arrives from the body. The brain processes it, including what similar experiences have felt like before. Then there is a response, such as taking the jumper off.

This process helps us understand our surroundings, keep track of our body and engage with what's happening. People process sensory information differently. A visible reaction is just one part of that process. Before judging the reaction, it helps to ask what information may have arrived, and how it might have felt.

### Slide 8

This framework from Winnie Dunn describes four patterns of sensory response. Registration means some input may not be picked up readily. Sensory seeking means actively looking for more input, perhaps through movement, touch or sound. Sensory sensitivity means noticing input strongly, even if the person doesn't act to change it. Sensory avoiding means actively reducing input, such as covering ears or choosing a quieter place.

A person may seek one kind of input and avoid another. The pattern can also shift with the setting and the load already being carried. Some families will have encountered a sensory profile through an occupational therapist. Use this as a way to notice patterns and ask questions. It doesn't give us a complete description of a young person.

Source and adaptation: Parents and Carers - Secondary, Session 2, slide 8; matching slide PDF page 8; PC Secondary - Facilitator Guide PDF page 33; full speaker notes. Authored audience-facing delivery, with optional participation and no invented participant responses.

### Slide 9

Stimming can include pacing, tapping, fidgeting, humming or repeating a favourite sound. It may help your young person settle, focus, cope or express joy. Some teenagers hide their stims at school to fit in, and that can take energy.

Notice what stimming does for them, and make room for it. Fidget tools or music may help, depending on their preferences. Respect privacy too. Some young people want space to stim without others watching. If a movement causes harm, look for a safer way to meet the same need. Follow your young person's lead rather than making their expression another thing to monitor.

### Slide 12

The detail-first idea describes building understanding from individual pieces towards a whole picture. Someone may notice a small change, an inconsistency or a background detail before the overall situation becomes clear.

This can be connected with strengths such as pattern recognition, deep memory, logical precision, honesty and close attention. Think about what your young person knows in detail, or what they notice that other people miss. These strengths are present in everyday life.

Taking in a lot of detail can also take effort. A task may be hard to begin if the larger picture or first step is unclear. The same way of processing can bring depth and also create demands. We can notice both without turning this into a checklist that every young person must fit.

### Slide 13

Think about your own attention for a moment. Does it move between several things, or become deeply absorbed in one? The torch and floodlight image gives us two ways to picture that. Monotropism describes attention going deeply into fewer things at a time. Autistic people help develop this way of understanding attention.

Deep interests can offer learning, memory and joy. When your young person is absorbed in a game, a drawing, a favourite topic or a song, their attention may be deeply invested. Asking them to stop for dinner can involve real effort. Warning, time and a helpful transition can make a difference. That absorbed state, sometimes called flow, is also something worth protecting.

### Slide 14

Executive functioning is a name for processes we use to organise and carry out everyday tasks. The slide includes planning, organisation, time management and working memory. It also includes thinking about our thinking, managing emotions, flexibility, attention, getting started and persisting.

We use different combinations of these throughout the day. When a task is hard, identifying the particular demand can help us find useful support. Let's look at getting started and switching focus, because they ask different things of a person. Secondary school can add a timetable, multiple teachers, homework and assessment dates to keep track of. A capable young person can still need support with starting or planning. Executive demands and subject knowledge are different things.

### Slide 20

Interoception concerns signals from inside the body: temperature, hunger, thirst or needing the bathroom. Alexithymia describes difficulty identifying and describing emotions and feelings. They're related ideas, but noticing a body signal and knowing how to describe an emotion are different tasks.

A young person might notice hunger only when it becomes intense, or find it hard to tell whether a fluttery feeling before a school camp or party is excitement or worry. These examples give us questions to explore, rather than a diagnosis. We can offer time and ways to communicate what is happening inside, without insisting on an immediate answer.

### Slide 21

Sometimes a build-up is happening inside even when the surface doesn't show it. Early signs may be subtle, individual or simply unavailable to us. This is an invitation to learn together, never a reason to blame yourself for not noticing sooner.
