# Parents and Carers - Secondary · Session 4

## Home and Community

### Slide 1

Welcome back. This morning we built our understanding of Autistic experience. Now we consider everyday life with a teenager: distress and recovery, homework, interests, growing independence, sleep and screens.

You know your family, and your young person has their own knowledge and preferences too. Take what fits and leave what doesn't. We are looking for workable supports and small steps you can consider together.

### Slide 2

What we can see and hear may be only the tip of the iceberg. Underneath can sit thoughts, feelings, values and needs, including belonging, predictability, security and self-expression.

Think privately of a hard moment. What had happened beforehand? Perhaps there was an unexpected change, a long day of different teachers and rooms, or a request that was simply too big for that day. We may not know the answer straight away. Behaviour can give us information, and curiosity helps us look beyond the surface. This isn't about blaming a young person or a parent. It is about understanding the conditions around a difficult moment. With teenagers, distress may show up as withdrawal, anger or a door being slammed. Keep the same curiosity about the load underneath.

### Slide 3

Overwhelm can turn inward or outward. A shutdown may look like becoming very still, freezing, withdrawing to a room or being less able to speak and explain what is needed. A meltdown may involve crying, running away or a large outward expression of distress.

These are involuntary responses to overwhelm. Quietness can be easy to miss, especially when someone seems to be managing. The response alone may not tell us the full story. Keep the person's safety and need for reduced demands in view, and allow some space around these experiences. They can be hard for families to revisit.

### Slide 4

Sometimes overload builds over a long period. Autistic burnout describes deep physical, mental and emotional exhaustion, with a reduction in capacity that may last. School Can't describes being unable to attend because school has become overwhelming through anxiety, fear or sensory load.

These terms help us consider what someone may have been carrying and what support or recovery they need. They don't let us diagnose an individual situation from a slide. If your family is living through this, it can be very hard. A conversation with appropriate health and school supports can help explore the individual picture. We'll look at working with school in Session Five.

### Slide 5

In the moment, try reducing input: fewer words, fewer questions and less noise. A quiet, safe space works best when you've agreed it with your young person beforehand, while everyone is calm. Keep your approach matched to what you know helps your young person.

A distressed person may have very little capacity for explanation or problem-solving. The conversation about what happened can wait until they are ready. On page twelve, there's room to gather early signs, what helps in the moment and what helps recovery. You can add to that picture over time. These are options to consider, rather than a script every family has to follow.

### Slide 6

Co-regulation means a trusted adult helping someone settle through a steady, responsive presence. Teenagers can still need that support, although it may look different now. It might be sitting nearby, a quiet drive or being available without many words. Respect their need for space.

Self-regulation involves the ways a person manages their own energy and feelings, with support that develops over time. Nobody stays calm every time, especially when exhausted. Your own capacity matters too. We can look for support without expecting either you or your young person to manage perfectly.

### Slide 7

An after-school crash can continue into the teenage years and sometimes become more noticeable as the demands grow. A young person may appear to manage at school, then arrive home exhausted or go straight to their room. Both accounts can be real parts of the same day.

Recovery might involve food, quiet, movement, a screen or time with an interest. Let questions and homework wait until there is more capacity. A predictable routine or a quiet car ride may help.

Connection may happen later, through a shared game, a drive or a conversation in the evening. Give space without disappearing from their support network. If withdrawal does not ease or you are concerned about wellbeing, seek support to understand what is happening for them.

### Slide 8

Homework brings subject work and the demands of starting, planning and keeping track. Agree together when it will happen, allowing for recovery, and where it works best. Sometimes a person sitting nearby while doing their own task makes the start easier.

Make due dates and the steps between them visible in a format your young person can use. Break a larger task into smaller parts with a clear finish. This can support the planning while the work stays theirs.

If the load repeatedly exceeds their capacity, bring that information to school and ask about adjustments. Families often do a great deal of organising already. Look for a small support that makes the task more manageable for both of you.

### Slide 9

Interests can be an anchor for regulation, connection and joy. Time with a familiar interest may help restore energy. It can also offer a way into your young person's world: listening while they explain, watching what they make or joining in when invited.

An interest is part of their experience and identity. Taking it away as a consequence can also take away a source of regulation. If that approach has been familiar at home, you can consider the function the interest serves and what another response might look like. Stay curious about what it gives your young person. For many teenagers, an interest is also where their friends and online communities are. You don't have to be good at their game to show interest in what it means to them.

### Slide 10

Independence can grow in small, supported steps. There is no birthday by which everyone has to do the same things. The useful step might involve an alarm, a little money, part of a journey or communicating a preference at an appointment.

Talk about risk together. What feels safe enough? What support stays in place, and what happens if the plan changes? Support can change as confidence grows, and it can remain where it is needed. Home can continue to be a safe base where your young person can be themselves while exploring more of the world.

### Slide 11

Sleep and screens can raise difficult questions for families. Tiredness affects capacity, and some Autistic young people find sleep hard. At the same time, screens can offer rest, regulation, interests and real connection.

Look at what the screen is doing for your young person as well as what worries you. What matters most for your family right now? Work towards an agreement together, and choose a time to review how it is going. A general rule doesn't tell us the whole picture. Keep concerns about sleep, wellbeing or online safety open for appropriate support, while making room for your young person's perspective.

### Slide 13

Your own battery counts too. Helping another person settle is harder when you are running on empty. On page thirteen, take a moment with My own battery. What small thing helps you recharge? Who, if anyone, could help make this week a little lighter?

It might be a short walk, a few quiet minutes, a conversation or practical help with a task. If support is scarce, that is a real constraint, not something you have done wrong. Keep the idea realistic. Pause here to think or write.

### Slide 13

Keep whatever feels possible. Looking after your capacity is part of family planning. Sometimes the useful step is reducing one demand or asking for specific help. It says nothing about how much you love your young person, and it doesn't have to become another task to succeed at.
